Vedic dialectics as the quintessence of Vedism has exerted an enormous influence on the neighboring Chinese civilization, having the greatest philosophical appeal to the Chinese thinkers of the era which have adopted it in the form of adhv ̄ac ̄ara or the “Doctrine of the Way”, widely known by its Chinese simplified spelling Daoism, viz.
道教dàojiào “Teachings of the Dao”, or 道家 dàojiā School of the Dao”.
Some Rig-Vedic hymns specify an Ā ditya (a monthly or seasonal aspect of the Sun) called P ̄us ̣an as the heavenly “plower“, e.g., in the Rig-Veda I 23.15 P ̄us ̣an is invoked by the poet to keep driving s ̣aḍ yuktā n̐ the “six yoked ones” as if plowing an arable field with oxen, metaphorically denoting ̥rtū n “6 seasons of the year” (viz. ś iś ira thawing, vasanta spring, grī s ̣ma summer, vars ̣ā rain, ś arad autumn/fall, hemanta winter). Thus, undoubtedly, P ̄us ̣an is metaphorically equated with a plower who plows the 6 seasons of the Year as an arable field. Hence, undoubtedly P ̄us ̣an himself was authentically conceived as Prajā pati “Lord of the 6 Seasons“ or Purus ̣a “Spirit/Breather” the primeval cosmic being, the embodiment of the Year, viz. cyclical Time Kā la, of which the Year is the main cosmic unit.
This Vedic concept of Ā ditya P ̄us ̣an as the embodiment of the Year is supported by his title Pū s ̣an pathas-pati “Lord of the paths“, identified with adhvan the “Way“ itself (Rig-Veda VIII 31.11), conceived as having two dialectically opposite parts, sides, aspects (Rig-Veda VI 58.1) regarded as the two mouths of the Year (Taittirī ya Ā ran ̣yaka I 2.3-4), viz. ś uklam Bright side vs yajatam synonymous with k̥rs ̣ṇam Dark side, and is further strenghtened with the epithet ā gh̥rṇi (a cognate with Avestan Zuruuan “Time, viz. god of growth, maturity and decay“; but Vedic term is composed from the prefix ā - + the root √Gh ̥r < IE *Gu̯her + suffix ni), in a sequential relationship, viz. Pū s ̣an ā gh ̥rni (once as the epithet of Agni the Fire, sometimes also without prefix as Gh̥rni and Gh̥rṇi-vat), and as such was borrowed in its integral form by the ancient Chinese, known as Pan Gu in Chinese and identified with the Tao (Wade romanization) or Dao (Pinyin), whereby the Sanskrit terminology being downsized to monosyllables as to conform to the monosyllabic structure of the Chinese language, viz.:
