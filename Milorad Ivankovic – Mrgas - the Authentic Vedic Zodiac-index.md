The Greek Zodiac translated misleadingly as rā ś i “heap” in Sanskrit was adopted by the Hindus through their cultural interrelations with the Yavanas (viz. Greeks) after the campaign of Alexander the Great to India in the 4th century BCE. Thus it is believed that the ancient Indo-Aryans of the Vedic era, though they applied the Lunar Zodiac of initially 28 and later 27 Lunar mansions, did not make use of the Solar Zodiac.
Apparently there must have occurred a certain gap or considerable discontinuity in the long line of oral transmission of the Vedic tradition among the native Indo-Aryans, since they are not even aware of the existence of an authentic archaic Vedic generic term for the designation of asterisms and constellations of the Solar Zodiac, and though well preserved in the oldest parts of the Rig-Veda, it is not recognized by the native Indian scientists and scholars abroad as well.
That original autochthonous Vedic term appears to be m̥rga denoting “beasts or wild animals” (synonymous with Greek ζῷον [zō ion] “animal” from which derived the term Zodiac itself), but its real usage has remained completely unrecognized by modern exegetes. However, since there exist neither “beasts nor animals” in heaven, the Vedic term m̥rgá could have only designated “asterisms or constellations“, or more precisely the semblance of such creatures actually projected by human imagination onto heavenly imagery. Thus undoubtedly the ancient Vedans did make themselves use of the Solar Zodiac and various m̥rgas perceivable on the infinite heavenly screen at sunset and night-time, as explicitly specified in the case of god Varun ̣a (see below).
In the context of the ancient Vedic Solar Zodiac of special significance are the members of the divine group collectively called Ā dityas (the “Suns“), for the ritual texts . The term is derived from the name of the goddess A-diti, usually translated as “In-nocence, Non-guiltiness, viz. Guiltlessness“ in socio-ethical manner currently in vogue by the western indologists, originally synonymous with the New Year’s Dawn Us ̣as, associated with the Bright aspects of heaven (opposite to Diti, the mother of 7 Maruts the stormy wind gods, or interpreted by some as meteorites; Hillebrandt presumes that it might have been an older masculine term for the 7 Pleiades; according to Hewitt they represent 7 stars of the Great Bear constellation), having a precise astronomical meaning “Un-erring, Im-peccable, Non-transgressive“ in her announcing of the beginning of New Year (viz. the New Year’s Day) with the succession of the specific monthly and seasonal aspects of the Sun (backed up in its appearance at sunset [acronycal setting] and in the morning [heliacal rising] against related stars, asterisms and constellations in the background.
Hence, Ā dityas the sons of Aditi, are associated rather with the West in Vedic texts, viz. with the acronycal setting of the constellations at sunset, e.g. the sovereign god Varun ̣a the king of the universe is associated with the western region as its overlord, and with the serpent P ̥rdaku as its Protector (Atharva-Veda XII 3.57).
Thus, Varun ̣a is often mentioned with the Sun at sunset, either alone (as in Kauśītaki Br ̄ahman ̣a XVIII 9), and Rig-Veda VII 87.6 where he is directly called m ̥rga “a Zodiacal constellation“;



or together with his mutually opposite partner Mitra (Rig-Veda V 62.1) where the swift horses of the Sun they use to unloose, in accord with the hidden order ( ̥rta viz. path of the Sun along the ecliptic against the background of the Zodiacal stars) at the end of the day, but also at the end of the year, since the individual day is also the image of the Year (Aitareya Br ̄ahman ̣a III 39.6).
For the earth in 24 h makes a complete round through all the constellations of the Zodiac circle, just as the Sun does in the Year time-span.
The ancient Greeks borrowed the same term from the Asia Minor Aryans (originally the *Mitranis < Mitra, the cuneiform incorrect spelling Mi-ta-an-ni) and adopted it in the form Τιτάν (*tito < ā dityo “the Sun“, for there are 12 ̄adityas-months in the Veda, and consequently 12 Titans in Greek tradition), assuming erroneously that the initial long ā vowel (viz. prefix to the word) represented a preposition, hence decomposed it while dropped the prefix.
The above identity explains the relationship of Vedic Varun ̣a with Greek Ouranos (both meaning “Bond“ or in some context “Binding“) since they originally belong to Titans or Ā dityas. Moreover, it is explicitly stated that Varun ̣a is viś va-darś ata viz. “visible to all“ (Rig-Veda I 25.18), which testifies undeniably of his material non-abstract astral character, called m̥rga “a Zodiacal constellation“ (Rig-Veda VII 87.6), naturally being visible when adhi-pā ́ “over-lord“ is andhá “darkness“, i.e. at night (Rig-Veda VII 88.2). And Varun ̣a himself like Ouranos is identified with Night and Darkness.
Some Rig-Vedic hymns specify an Ā ditya (a monthly or seasonal aspect of the Sun) called P ̄us ̣an as the heavenly “plower“, e.g. in the Rig-Veda I 23.15 P ̄us ̣an is invoked by the poet to keep driving s ̣aḍ yuktā n̐ the “six yoked ones” as if plowing an arable field with oxen, metaphorically denoting ̥rtū n “6 seasons of the year” (viz. ś iś ira thawing, vasanta spring, grī s ̣ma summer, vars ̣ā rain, ś arad autumn/fall, hemanta winter). Thus, undoubtedly, P ̄us ̣an is metaphorically equated with a plower who plows the 6 seasons of the Year as an arable field. Hence, undoubtedly P ̄us ̣an ̄aghrni (viz. asterism or constellation Sirius viz. “burning, scorching, searing one“) himself was authentically conceived as the embodiment of the Year, viz. cyclical Time, of which the year is the main cosmic unit.
In addition, his brother god Indra, the heavenly Bull (viz. the constellation of Taurus) chief of the gods during the Age of Taurus and an Ā ditya too, is especially hailed as Sī ra-pati “Lord of the plow“ and Sī tā the “Furrow“ as his wife, during the Ś un ̄asīriya festival, an annual sacrifice of Vedic agriculturists.
Thus all the materials collected, point to the Sun as the heavenly “plower“, for the Sun like a plower makes a furrow straight ahead across the sky, daily from east to west, and yearly along the ecliptic against the background of various m ̥rgas “beasts or animals“ (cf. Rig-Veda X 136.6 genitive plural m ̥rgā nā m; the term being applied to the chief gods, e.g. m̥rga Vis ̣ṇu, m̥rga Varuṇa, m̥rga Prajā pati or V̥rs ̣ā kapi but also to the demons m̥rga Ś us ̣ṇa, m̥rga V̥rtra, etc.:



the famous Vedic etymologist Y ̄aska (7.c. BCE) in his Nirukta XIII 3 explains m̥rga quite correctly as ā ditya viz. the Sun (of a specific month or season of the year), a Vedic term denoting asterisms or constellations (though still unrecognized by modern Western scholars which tend to see in Vedic gods nothing else but only abstract socioethical concepts) in the belt of the Zodiac specified by the term ̥rtá “(cosmic) order, or orderly path (of the Sun, and the Moon and of the seasons, according to the Yearly timeschedule)“, usually translated in a socio-ethical manner as “truth“, by overlooking its astronomical significance completely (thus crippling the Veda to the level of slobbery mawkish sentimental childlish Santaclaus-like stories preached by the false prophets Buddha, Zarathustra and Jeezus), for Veda is “Science“ with capital “S“.
The Lunar Zodiac lies along the Solar Zodiacal belt, but is made up of twenty seven or twenty eight parts identified by the same number of lunar asterisms. Significantly, the authentic Vedic Zodiac did include also asterisms outside from the ordinary Zodiac belt of 12 Greek signs, viz. the Pleiades, Orion, Sirius, Canopus, Argo Navis, Hydra, Prokyon, Corvus, Ophiuchus, 7 stars of the Great Bear, the circumpolar Draco constellation and the Pole Star (naturally under specific Vedic names).

However, the terms m ̥rgá ś iras (variants m̥rgaś ī rs ̣á and m̥rgayú ) and Rohinị̄ as well (along with K̥rttikas) are completely unknown to the Rig-Veda, they belong to the later Naks ̣atra system of Moon’s night abodes (viz. Besides, the ā rdrā (Betelgeuse star) in the right shoulder of Orion is already counted as a separate Lunar asterism, appearing on the list immediately after m̥rgá ś iras or M ̥rgayú.
