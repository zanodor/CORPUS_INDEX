The Aryan Swan-symbolism
in Etruscan and Celtic Mythology
The concept of haṃsa or literally “Swan” was a powerful psychological symbol of the
Vedic Aryan worldview, and as such it continues to be revered with the same piety in
India to the present time. It is quite curious, however, that the Swan-symbolism along
with the noble Aryan swastikas prevail on the artifacts and remnants of Etruscan era,
especially in the light of the current unanimously accepted theory on Etruscans as
autochthonously European and a non-Indo-European speaking people.

The word हंस haṃ sa is a Vedic Sanskrit term meaning “white swan or goose” (it is
actually a cognate with the English “goose”) being symbolic of human viz. Individual
Soul, its transcendental quality and sinless purity.
In Hinduism the term often appears compounded with the adjective परम parama
“highest, supreme” viz. Paramahaṃsa “Supreme sc. celestial Swan” as the
designation of the Creator god Brahma the Universal Soul. Incidentally, the same
term paramahaṃsa is also applied as a theological honorific title in addressing Hindu
spiritual teachers of the highest order called sannyasin “renunciate” who are believed
to have attained the union with the supreme Universal Soul.
The Aryan Vedic worldview has conceived the haṃsa < Indo-European *ǵ hans
“swan, goose” (cf. Mayrhofer 1996:799) as symbolic for the human soul or spirit
released after death from a deceased person, typified by the speckless white color of
a swan or goose, and likewise assumed to be migratory like a goose (whence the
theory of metempsychosis or transmigration of the Soul).
What is more, the haṃsa is closely associated with and even considered the vā hana
or “vehicle of the god of the dead, as is recorded by various Hindu scriptures.
Although Varun ̣a the Vedic god of the dead in post-Vedic times seems to be
retitled as the Lord of the waters, he is anyway addressed with such an awe and dread
expressive only in the presence of the former god of death. The Vedic god Varun ̣̣a,
though later superseded by Yama, it was he and not Yama, who is propitiated by the
following formula associated with the White Swan:
sarva-ambhasā ṃ patir devo Varuṇo haṃsa-vā hanah ̣
pū jitah ̣pr ̄itimā n astu sa-samudra-nad ̄i-nadah ̣
viz. “Lord of all the waters, god Varuna, who has a (White) Swan as his vehicle, may
he who is thus worshipped be propitious to us, with ocean, and with all the (male &
female) rivers and streams along.” (Nā tỵ aśā stra III 62-64).
Thus, there can be no doubt that in antiquity the Vedic Aryans considered “White
Swan” as symbolic of the soul or spirit of a deceased person.
By way of digression, in Hindu mythology haṃsa the White Swan is conceived
rather as the vā hana or vehicle of the Creator god Brahma the Universal Soul.



Surprisingly, the Swan as symbolic of the soul of a deceased person appears on the
Etruscan alabaster urn from Chiusi, with reclining male figure and kline-shaped chest
with inscription, first half of the 3rd century BCE, inv. 13897 - Museo Gregoriano
Etrusco - Vatican Museums (see below Figure 1).

Below the reclining figure, there is a relief with four carved figures, two oriented
left vs. two oriented right, the latter two being the mirror images of the former ones.
The text in Etruscan script incised beneath the relief begins with the sequence of
letters LAR. Regardless of the inscription incised below, the depiction of a Swan
coupled with a winged male figure, undoubtedly represents domestic guardian spirit
called Lar, and this can hardly be a mere coincidence.
The image clearly indicates the belief that Lar guards the soul of the deceased in
the form of a Swan. Whatsmore, the scene depicted does not show the Swan in a calm
spiritual attitude floating motionless on the surface of the water, but apparently one
making noise by flapping his wings as an accompaniment to the “singing of the
Swan” better known as “Swan Song”, being actually a metaphor for death. For the
phrase “Swan Song” refers in fact to an ancient belief that the Swan sings a beautiful
song just before his death, though being mostly silent during his whole lifetime.
Interestingly, this belief had become proverbial in ancient Greece by the 3rd
century BCE about the same period of the date of the Etruscan urn from Chiusi. The
famous Greek writer Aesop in his fable of The Swan and the Goose mentions the
Swan Song legend as saving the very life of the bird, when it was caught by mistake
instead of the goose, but was recognized by its song.
Even more instructive is Plato’s Phaedo, for it quotes the words of Socrates, which
actually confirm the common Aryan belief in metempsychosis or Transmigration of
the Soul: “[84] And you seem to think I am inferior in prophetic power to the swans who sing at other times also, but when they feel that they are to die, [85a] sing most and best in their joy that they are to go to the god whose servants they are. But men, because of their own fear of death, misrepresent the swans and say that they sing for sorrow, in mourning for their own death. They do not consider that no bird sings when it is hungry or cold or has any other trouble; no, not even the nightingale or the swallow or the hoopoe which are said to sing in lamentation. I do not believe they sing for grief, nor do the swans; [85b] but since they are Apollo's birds, I believe they have prophetic vision, and because they have foreknowledge of the blessings in the other world they sing and rejoice on that day more than ever before.”
Thus the Transmigration of the Soul or metempsychosis as the quintessence of the
common Aryan and likewise Etruscan worldview is perfectly attested by the carved
relief on the Etruscan urn from Chiusi.



On mentioning LAR, it comes immediately to mind the ancient story of Irish king
Lir, since Etruscan Lar resembles king Lir not only phonetically but also
conceptually. For according to Irish tradition Lir was closely associated with the
Swans. The ancient Irish story relates how “many years ago in Ireland, lived a King
and ruler of the sea called Lir. He had a beautiful wife, called Eva, who gave him four
children – eldest son Aodh, a daughter called Fionnula, and twin boys, Fiachra and
Conn. When the children were young, their mother Eva died. Lir and children were very sad, and King wanted a new mother for his young sons and daughter, so he
married Eva’s sister Aoife who, it was said, possessed magical powers.
Aoife loved the children and Lir at first, but soon she became very jealous of the
time that King spent with Aodh, Fionnula, Fiachra, and Conn. She wanted to have all of his attention for herself. One day, she took the children to swim in a lake while
the sun was hot in the sky. When they got there and children took to the water,
Aoife used her powers to cast a spell over children, which would turn them all into
beautiful SWANS.
She knew that if she killed children, their ghosts would haunt her forever, so instead, she cast this spell, forcing them to live as swans for 900 years - three
hundred on Lake Derravaragh, three hundred on Straits of Moyle, and three
hundred more on Isle of Inish Glora. The spell would only be broken when children
heard the ringing of a bell, and the arrival of St. Patrick in Ireland.
But Aoife’s spell had not taken away children’s voices, and so it was that these
four beautiful SWANS could sing beautiful songs and were able to tell their father
what had happened to them. Lir, who had been searching for his children, came
down to the lake and saw Fionnuala, now a Swan, who told him of spell cast on
them by Aoife. Enraged, he banished Aoife into the mist, and she was never seen again.”
Despite somewhat different arrangements of mythical elements in the Irish story,
the powerful Aryan Vedic Swan-symbolism apparently pervaded both Irish and
Etruscan mythology equally and quite impressively.
In this context, of paramount importance is the fact verified abundantly by the
innumerable cremation urns, that the Etruscans, just like Vedic Aryans favored
cremation over inhumation of their deceased, which is another unmistakable proof of
the Aryan background of the Etruscan civilization, not to mention the frequent use of
the preeminently Aryan symbol swastika on Etruscan artifacts.
