According to Hindu yogic tradition, the word Haṭ ha is an encoded compound whereby ha means sū rya “Sun” and ṭ ha means candra “Moon”. Thus, allegedly Haṭ ha yoga designates the Sun-and-Moon yoga. However, this is illogical and erroneous, since the syllables Ha-Ṭ ha in yogic physiology correspond to the two main channels of breath in human body, viz. Iḍ ā and Piṅ galā , the former is associated with Left nostril,
whereas the latter is associated with Right nostril.
Thus, in reality the syllable Ha represents the MOON and Ṭ ha represents the SUN.
In fact, the Sanskrit sequence Ha-Ṭ ha translates precisely the widely spread and renown Chinese Daoist philosophical concept expressed by the compound term Yī n-Yá ng as symbolic for the dialectical unity of two opposite but complementary principles, viz. Shade vs. Light, Dark/Black vs. Bright/White, liquid vs. concrete, Water vs. Fire, cool
vs. warm, cold vs. hot, calm vs. excited, MOON vs. SUN, Left vs Right, etc.
Now, there arises quite a logical question: “How and why Haṭ ha Yoga came to be mistranslated and misinterpreted by the sequence “Sun-and-Moon yoga” instead of the correct form “MOON-and-SUN yoga ?
In order to attain the proper insight into the matter one should refer to the Da Zhuan
大傳 aka Xi Ci 繫辭 viz. “Great Commentary aka Appendix” the most famous ancient
philosophical appendage to the Yi Jing 易經 or the “Book of Changes”, the authorship
of which is assigned to the greatest Chinese scholar and philosopher Confucius Kǒng
fū zǐ 孔夫子 viz. “Master Kung”. There is recorded a full scale of precise explanations
on the subject, leaving no room for any possible misjudgment or misinterpretation.
As to the original meanings of Yin and Yang, it is given verbatim in the Section A, chapter 6th of the Xi Ci what is precisely meant by these two terms in Chinese:
阴 阳 之 义 配 日 月 = yī n yá ng zhī yì pè i rì yuè
viz. “The yin and yang in meanings correspond to Sun and Moon”.
In view of the less experienced or casual readers at first sight it may seem so obvious that there is a perfect congruence of the two sequences: yin - yang and Sun – Moon. Following the same logic, Yin should mean (be equated with) Sun, whereas Yang
should mean (be equated with) Moon.
However, just a little parsing of the sentence members will suffice to decode their true meanings.



A) Chinese character yī n 陰 (traditional) vs 阴 (modern simplified) conveys the
following meanings:
cloudy
shady
cool/cold
Yin (the negative principle of Yin and Yang)
negative (electricity)
feminine principle
Moon ( yuè 月)
B) Chinese character yá ng 陽 (traditional) vs 阳 (modern simplified) conveys the
following meanings:
bright
sunny
warm/hot
Yang (the positive principle of Yin and Yang)
positive (electricity)
male principle
Sun ( rì 日)
C) Chinese character yuè 月 = “Moon, month, monthly”
D) Chinese character rì 日 = “Sun, day, day of the month”
Thus there can be no any doubt as to what yin and yang actually correspond to, viz.
yīn 陰 = equal Moon yuè 月
yá ng 陽 = equal Sun rì 日



Now, the question is why the ancient Chinese did not follow strictly the proper sequence “MOON and SUN” when making correspondence to “yin and yang”, but used to mention Sun as first and Moon as second instead ?
There are several reasons for such incongruence:
1.)First, there is an error in the sequencing of the terms in Chinese language due to their
old Semitic-like custom of writing from right to left, thus yin appears first when viewed leftwards on the symbolic Yin-Yang taijitu diagram (see below).
Bright=Yang Dark=Yin
(POSITIVE) (NEGATIVE)
Hence the Chinese violate the usual Positive-Negative pattern of sequencing binominal compounds in the sequence yin-yang (instead of the correct Yang-Yin pattern), but maintain it when speaking of the Sun and the Moon (rì - yuè ).
2.) The chosen initial member of the compound terms as a rule is the shorter one, hence yin appears as first, and yang as second in the sequence.
3.) The Sun is the only primary source of light in our universe, hence it is given the priority over the Moon (a dead planet which has no light of its own butb only reflects
the oncoming lighwaves from the Sun) and other planets. That is why the speakers of Aryan (Indo-European tongues) and the Chinese likewise, put the Sun as first when mention the heavenly bodies, whence the standard pronunciation sequence has become “Sun and Moon”, and not “Moon and Sun”.



The medieval Yogins of India apparently misunderstood or at least forgot the initial authentic meanings of Ha-Ṭ ha syllables, hence they identified the sequence with the standard speech pattern “Sun and Moon”, just like Confucius and his disciples did it
almost two millennia before.
As for the modern followers of Haṭ ha yoga, they use to repeat what they have heard from the gurus and yoga instructors without questioning the validity of traditional but erroneous interpretations.
