Abstract
Did the Vedic Aryans experience a kind of close encounter or make at least an acquaintance with the astronauts from the outer space in remote antiquity? There are indeed recorded in the hymns of the Rig Veda some accounts of utilization of the spaceships and even a spectacular appearance of mysterious cosmic navy assembled from golden spaceships is specified explicitly as occurred
in those distant times. Tracking the delineated data farther into antiquity, the present research has arrived at a stunning discovery, and now it returns with the attainment of equally spectacular new insights on the subject. Among these, the paper solves the greatest Vedic enigma of all times. Collaterally, the paper contributes additional arguments contra theory of the Arctic origin of the Veda and the Vedans. At last but not insignificantly, the paper challenges established linguistic
dogmas of Indo-Europeanistics.

1.Introduction
Quite long ago B.G.Tilak keenly noticed: “In Rigveda X 63.10 we are told that the land of the blessed is to be reached by ‘the celestial ship with a good rudder’. The words in the original are daívīṃ nā́ vaṃ svaritrā́ m. Comparing these with the expression divyásya śúnah ‘a celestial dog’ in the Atharva Veda VI 80.3 and seeing that a celestial (divya) representation of Rudra is described in later works, it seems to me that we must to interpret the epithet to mean ‘celestial’ and not ‘divine’ “ (Tilak 1893:110).
NB. In point of fact, the above phrase in the Atharva Veda VI 80.3 reads śúno divyásya in that order, respectively.
Besides, the celestial (viz. heavenly) spaceship is associated also with the twin gods Aś vins who were invited at one occasion by their devotees to come ā́ no nāvā́ viz. “in the ship” to join them at the Soma-sacrifice (Rig Veda I 46.7-8).
On another occasion the Aś vins came swiftly to rescue with their cátasro nā́ vo “a fleet of four ships” in order to save the needy from distress (Rigveda I 182.6).

2.Captain of the Cosmic Navy
The most spectacular appearance of this kind, however, was the arrival of a mysterious cosmic navy witnessed by Bharadvāja Bārhaspatya one of the seven legendary Vedic seers whose impressions of the event are found recorded in Rig-Veda VI 58.3:
yā́ s te pūṣan nā́ vo antáḥ samudré hiraṇyáyīr antárikṣe cáranti tā́ bhir yāsi dūtyā́ ṃ sū́ ryasya
viz. “the golden (space)ships of thine, O Pū ṣ an, that move in the (celestial) ocean in the atmosphere, with these thou goest on an emission of the Sun”.
Apparently, the commander-in-chief viz. captain of this mysterious navy was the Vedic god
Pū ṣ an invoked by name. Traditionally Pū ṣ an is considered an Ā ditya viz. “Sun” and as such it is mentioned in the Nirukta 7.9. Moreover, Pū ṣ an is directly called Sūrya “the Sun” in the oldest Upanishads, viz. B ̥rhadā raṇyaka Upaniṣ ad V 15.1-2 and Ī ś a Upaniṣ ad 15-16 (cf. Ivankovic 2020d). However, in the verse cited above, Bharadvāja has made a clear distinction between
Pū ṣ an and the Sun itself. The function of Pū ṣ an is designated by the term dūtya “emission” < dūta “emissary, envoy, negotiator, messenger, ambassador (sc. of the Sun)”.
Then who was the god named Pū ṣ an in the times of the Rig-Veda? Pū ṣ an was not an ordinary
divinity like numerous insignificant gods in ancient world, for it is specially acknowledged by Bharadvāja in Rig -Veda VI 56.5:
ārā́ t pūṣann asi śrutáḥ viz. “O Pū ṣ an, thou art famous afar”.
3.Maytime
The unique character of Pū ṣ an (cf. Ivankovic 2017b, 2017c) is specified by the two exclusive epithets of his viz. Mah and Ā gh ̥rṇi (actually ā-GH ̥rṇi, see below). The former term Mah “Mighty, great, large” (related to Roman Maius) in a sequential relationship with the signifier Pus̩ t̩ imbhara “flower-bearer, bringing growth/prosperity” (Rig-Veda IV 3.7) designates him as the personification of the month of May, viz. Maytime (the time of flowering and conspicuous growth of vegetation, and overall prosperity perceivable all around in nature). The latter one Ā gh ̥rṇi a compound term formed by the preposition ā- “towards” prefixed to the substantive GH ̥rṇá or GH ̥ŕṇi viz. “burning heat (of the Sun or Fire)” anticipates gradual changes in his character towards the scorching heat of summer. It is used 15 times in the Rig-Veda, 13 times in the Vocative, often all by itself (cf. Rig-Veda VI 53.9; VI 55.1,3; etc.) apparently as a proper name, viz. as the second name of Pū ṣ an. A.Hillebrandt already pointed out that “this epithet is all the more important since it does not occur in the later literature – only once in the Atharva Veda (in the verse VII 9.2 borrowed actually from Rig-Veda X 17.5) – thus giving the impression of belonging to a remote antiquity” (Hillebrandt 1980/1927:208).

In those remote times the summer commenced for the Vedic Aryans on the First of May, and for the Romans, Greeks, and Celts too, as already B.G.Tilak correctly remarked (cf. Tilak 1903:396399). However, Tilak overlooked a very important fact, viz. that in Vedic times the year itself (and not only summer) commenced on the First of May and continued to be celebrated as the New Year’s Day in ancient Roman Republic until the year 222 BCE. The ancients used to count the years in terms of summers. Accordingly, Pū ṣ an was considered the Year-God in those days (Taittirī ya Ā raṇyaka I 2.3-4) and hence a god of totality (cf. Ivankovic 2019). For the Year as cyclical Time was homologized with sarva “All” by the Vedic seers (cf. also Gonda 1984:62).
4.The Vedic Stellar Saga
The same conclusion can be inferred from the well-known stellar saga of 3059 BCE starring (the deities as the real stars of heaven) viz. Uṣas as Rohiṇī (Aldebaran), Prajāpati as M ̥rgaśīrsa (the head of Orion) and Rudra as M ̥rgavyādha or Tiṣya the killer of Orion (allegedly Sirius, though rather of uncertain and disputable identity which see further below), (cf. Ivankovic 2017c).
The story narrates how Rudra the archer filled with anger pierced Prajā pati with the three-starred or three partite arrow (represented by the three stars in the belt of Orion) and among his other deeds he knocked out Pū ṣ an’s teeth with the end of his bow. Hence, Pū ṣ an came to be known as deva karūḷatin “toothless god” subsequently in the ritual called karambhād “oatmeal-eater” (for having no teeth to chew hard food), both terms being obviously symbolic of the arrival of the warm season, the central and most comfortable period of the year when the winter (sun) finally lost its biting power (since in Vedic times the year had only three seasons, divided into four months each called caturmāsya).
NB. This was in sharp contrast with the god Varuna (also an Ā ditya) called deva jumbaka or viklidha viz. “the bucktoothed god” (Taittirī ya Brā hmaṇa III 9.3.15; Ś atapatha Brā hmaṇa XIII 3.6.5) as the personification of the biting power of the winter [sun] (Maitrā yaṇī Saṃ hitā I 10.12; Kā ṭ haka Saṃ hitā 36.6); (cf. Ivankovic 2020a, 2020b).
As for Rudra, there are serious objections against his identification with Tiṣya or the star Sirius. However, Tiṣya (of which a cognate is Avestan Tištriia) it could have not represented the star Sirius since this very term was derived from the Vedic compound tri-st ̥r viz. “three stars” originally denoting the tripartite arrow (thrown by Rudra at Prajā pati) represented by the three stars in the belt of Orion (correctly deciphered by Tilak 1893:127; cf. also Mayrhofer 1992:649, ir. (=Indo-Iranian) *tistriia-, im Aia. (=Old Indo-Aryan) dissimiliert zu *tisrija- > ved. tisíya- ; Etymon *tri-str-iio - 'der zum Dreigestirn Gehörig). Tilak also argued that “Rudra is the presiding deity of Ā rdrā (viz. Betelgeuse star in Orion) and we may therefore suppose Rudra to be represented by the star in the right shoulder of Orion” (Tilak 1893:102). Tilak further noticed that Rudra is known by “his appearance as Kirāta or hunter” (Tilak 1893:124) and “that he seems to be described as followed by dogs or rather as their master (Vaj. San. 16.27)”

Besides, autumn is his season, cf. 5.The Scorching One
Thus, irrespectively, whether was Rudra represented by the asterism Ā rdrā (=Betelgeuse) or by the three stars in the belt of Orion, or eventually by the constellation Sagittarius, the star Sirius must have represented someone else, and that could not have been anyone but Pū ṣ an the toothless god. This assumption is verified by the following three uncontestable proofs:
1) In the year 3059 BCE when the Vedic stellar saga took place the star Aldebaran was aligned in her heliacal rising with the vernal equinox, while Sirius appeared in his heliacal rising on the horizon (40 days ahead of Aldebaran on the ecliptic) precisely on the eve of May in the same year. The appearance of Sirius, viz. Pū ṣ an on the eve of May (after the disappearance of Orion), announcing the sailing-season when the auspicious winds began to blow in the Mediterranean, was the sign to the seafarers of the commencement of save and secure journeying. In ancient Rome Orion was associated with winter and inauspicious dangerous winds on the sea. The works of the classical Roman writers indisputably testify that the presence of saevus Orion viz. “fierce Orion” (Virgil, Aeneis VII 719) was considered nautis infesturs Orion turbaret hibernum mare “as long as Orion, the sailors’ foe should toss the wintry sea” (Horace, Epodon XV 7) inauspicious and hostile to the sailors (cf. Hillebrandt 1980/1927:289, 436, note 104).

The ancient “Swift Navy” or Argo Navis came to be divided in the 18th century by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille into 3 modern constellations called Carina (the hull), Puppis (the poop deck) and Vela (the sails). However, though Bharadvāja did not specified the exact number of the ships in the navy, the Rig-Vedic verse I 182.6 (associated with the Aś vin twins) cited previously above recognized precisely cátasro nā́ vo “a fleet of four ships”, the fourth of which might have been either Pyxis or even Antlia (see Figure 1 below).

6.The Egyptian Cognates
In ancient Egypt Sirius called Sopt was the most significant star in view of the Egyptian priestsastronomers, since its heliacal rising marked the inundation of the Nile, and accordingly with the commencement of the New Year (analogously as with the ancient Vedans and Romans, or rather their ancestors Trojans from Asia Minor). Among the common Egyptian-Vedic divinities, the goddess Isis (a cognate of Vedic Uṣas) identified with Aldebaran star (=Vedic Rohiṇī) was considered mistress of the celestial dog Sopt, since “Aldebaran residing in the constellation of Taurus, on its acronycal setting in the West was followed by Sirius or Sopt just as dog should follow his mistress” (Gržetić 1900:115). Of special significance in this context is the fact that there exists in South Slavic languages the verb soptati “to puff, viz. to breathe audibly in short quick breaths like a dog” (apparently of the same origin as the Egyptian signifier Sopt) which is a semantic equivalent of the Slavic verb puhati “to breathe audibly in short quick breaths in the manner of dogs” (related to Pūṣan > pūṣ/puṣ as explained above).
Contrary to the established linguistic dogmas, the Anatolians (Hittites, Luvians, Palaians, etc.), Albanians and Armenians had substantially nothing in common with the Aryans and the Veda, whereas the ancient Egyptians shared with the Vedans all the names and concepts of the chief divinities common to both Egyptian and Vedic Sanskrit (cf. Ivankovic 2017a): 1) Isis (Aldebaran in Taurus) = Uṣas (Dawn as Rohini)̣̄ ; 2) Osiris (Antares in Scorpio) = Father Asura; 3) Horus (God of Summer, see further below) = (ā)GH ̥rṇi; 4) Šyu (atmosphere, sky) = Dyu (sky); 5) Ptah Father the Creator God (conflated with Ra the Sun in the form of Scarabeus sacer; originally Osiris Ptah as single expression matching exactly Vedic Asura Pitah naḥ ‘Asura Father of ours’) = Avestan Ptar/Sanskrit Pitar > Pitaḥ ‘father’ (with devoicing of the final r into ̣ḥ in pausa); 6) Nut (Starry Night Sky) = Nakta, Greek Nux, Italian Notte, French Nuit; 7) Riʕ ‘Sun’ a common old proto-lexeme of special significance (cuneiform spelling Ri-a; rendered into Greek as Ra) = as final member of the Vedic compound Sūrya < *Sū-ria with prefix > *sua/sū ‘good, well’ + ̥r ‘moving straight ahead’ extended into ria, with Vedic suffix -ya, whence possessive compound ‘the one of good movement, the well-moving one, viz. the Sun’; incidentally, the same term might well be related even to the Proto-Chinese Ri 日 ‘Sun’, notwithstanding the given modern variant reconstructions of Old Chinese spelling of this character as *C.nik (by Baxter-Sagart 2014:93), or *nit (by Zhou Jixu 2002:4, in relationship with Latin niteo, nitere ‘to shine’).
The Riʕ/Ria ‘Sun’ is further related to the Vedic cosmic and ethic concept of ̥rta, quite correctly decoded long ago by Tilak (1893:157): “the path of ̥rta (Rig-Veda I 41.4) which is mentioned several times in the Rigveda, where the Ā dityas are said to be placed (X 85.1), and wherein Saramā discovered the cows of Indra (V 45.7,8) refers to the same broad belt of the Zodiac which the luminaries, as observed by the Vedic bards, never transgressed. It was so to speak their ‘right’ way, and therefore called ̥rta which though literally derived from ̥r, to go, soon came to mean the ‘right’ path, the circle of which exists for ever, or rather exists and exists (varvarti) in the vault of the heavens (Rig-Veda I 164.11)”.

Furthermore, the American mathematician A.Seidenberg demonstrated that the elements of ancient geometry found in Egypt stem from a ritual system of the kind found in the Vedic mathematical śulba-sūtras, e.g. the algorithm for building the pyramid-shaped funeral altar of the god Prajā pati (Seidenberg 1962, 1978, 1983) as the embodiment of the Year, viz. 7.The Psychopompos and the Celestial River
Pū ṣ an is frequently mentioned together with the Sarasvatī, a river endowed with special holiness, as in Rig-Veda X 17 (the hymn devoted to the Manes) where Pū ṣ an appears (in verse 3) as psychopompó s a conductor of souls to the other world, a knowing guide for the dead out of this world. Hillebrandt noticed that “the verse 8 is particularly important because it states that the Manes invoke Sarasvatī...it shows that the Sarasvatī is an object of worship for the Manes...According to the ritual of the Ś aunakins, after the funeral pyre is lit some offerings are made to Yama (the god of the dead) and then some to Sarasvatī . Here she is the stream of the dead. We may recall that the departed souls have to cross a river on their way to the other world. In later period this is the Vaitaraṇī, the ghorā nadī (=venerable river) which divides the dead from the living and flows in the sky as well as on the earth.

The Veda does not know the Vaitaraṇī yet: its place is taken by the Sarasvatī in the present hymn. Thus a clear picture emerges, in vv.3-6 Pū ṣ an conducts the departed one safely out of this world: vv.7-9 invoke the river that separates the dead from the living” (Hillebrandt 1980/1929:209-212).
Quite earlier A.Bergaigne (1883:47) hypothesized the Sarasvatī as a celestial river: “L’oiseau divin qui est à la fois le fœtus des eaux et des plantes, et qui ne peut être par conséquent qu’Agni ou Soma, reçoit au vers 1,164,52, le nom de Sarasvat, auquel correspond le féminin Sarasvatī, nom de la rivière céleste par excellence.”
Hillebrandt pursued the hypothesis further and identified the river thus: “Sarasvatī is closely associated with Sarasvat (Rig-Veda VII 95, 96). In these two hymns Sarasvatī is called ‘the divine one among the streams’ and obviously denotes not only one of the terrestrial streams but also a spirit of this river, i.e., its prototype in the sky. Thus I have no doubt that Sarasvatī cannot signify a phase of the moon...but only the celestial stream, that is to say, the Milky Way” (Hillebrandt 1980/1927:233).
Taking into account Pū ṣ an’s identity with Sirius (located on the right, viz. bright bank of the Milky Way) it emerged quite naturally his function as psychopompos. For he had to transport the deceased across the celestial river, viz. the Milky Way, to the other side into the dark realm of Yama, the god of the dead.
NB. Tilak too noticed the connection between the departed souls and the Milky Way, “In the later Indian literature we are told that the souls of the deceased have to cross a stream (called Vaitaraṇī, the Garuda Purā ṇa, Pretak, VI 25-31) before they reach the region of Yama, while the story of Charon shows that even the Greeks entertained a similar belief.” Though Tilak conflated the river Vaitaraṇī with the Milky Way, he was unaware of its older Vedic identification with the holy river Sarasvatī (Tilak 1893:109).
And the following Tilak’s remarks are interesting in this context, for he compared the similar beliefs of non-Aryan people from North America which also conceived “the Milky Way as ‘the Path of Spirits’, and ‘the Road of the Souls’, where they travel to the land beyond the grave, and where their camp fires may be seen blazing as brighter stars.” Tilak maintained further that “this coincidence between the Aryan and the non-Aryan legends makes it highly probable that the figures of the constellations were conceived by the Aryans according to notions of the next world prevailing amongst them at that time”. Nevertheless, he also pointed out one significant difference between the Aryan and the non-Aryan religious concepts: “It may be noticed, however, that the non-Aryan races do not connect the idea of Time, e.g., of the year and the seasons, with these beliefs, while it is the chief characteristic of the Aryan legends. 8.Contra Arctic Theory
In 1903 B.G.Tilak quite enthusiastically proposed his theory of the Arctic home of the Vedic Aryans and the interglacial or even pre-glacial origin of the Veda. However, the religion of Pū ṣ an and the twin gods Aś vins had nothing to do with the North Pole and Circumpolar Region, but rather it reflected (with its emphasis on [celestial] ocean journeying and utilization of the Cosmic Swift Navy or a fleet of four swift [space]ships) the conditions and environment of those living in the Mediterranean, the Black Sea area and Asia Minor (remember that the ancient Romans were in fact the Trojans, viz. the descendants of the people of Troy from Asia Minor).
