Romanian and Italian dialects
We will compare, in the lines below, the phonetics and the vocabulary of the Romanian language, and the Italian dialects. Some languages – Ladino, Friulano (Furlano) and Sardinian may be enumerated, for the sake of brevity, along the dialects.
Our comparison will be a phonetic one and a lexical one. We will not attempt a morphological comparison. Comparing the vocabulary of Romanian and Italian dialects is important, as some words in spoken, popular Latin were not written. Just comparing Romanian to classical Latin, and classical Latin to Italian dialects, will leave many words without an etymology.
Ladin has a Celtic influence *falce > fauz , cf. Fr. falsus > faux ; capra – ciaura ; cambiare > cangià
I. Phonetics.
It will not be always easy to distinguish an old shared phonetical transformation from an independent parallel evolution. (Rom. apă, limbă, Sard. abba, limba seem to be simple coincidences, independent innovations.
I.1. Consonants
Romanian and northern dialects (Piemontese, Lombardo, Veneto, Furlan) do not have double consonants, while the southern dialects (central, meridional, Sicilian, Sardinian) have them.

I. Vocabulary
Italian has kept a permanent relation with Latin, either through Church or the University. Latin is an adstrate of Italian. Some inherited words are kept in dialects, while Italian has adopted some Latinate loanword.
a) some names ( a piece, pezzo ; boy, ragazzo; un poco, a little ) and verbs (to take, prendere; ) are present in our list not for the sake of comparison, but to show the diversity of the synonyms used. These words are frequent in the language, but their synchronic frequence is not proportional with their diachronic durability. It is as the language has decided to use for some concepts (name, foot, moon, water, star, night) everlasting names, and to use disposable words for other concepts.
b) Week-day names (lunedi, martedi, mercoledi) have been influenced by Germanic (Mon-tag, Diens-tag, Wednes-tag) ; Romanian, Spanish, some Italian dialects keep an older form. dies and *diurnu (northern di, Sard. die) have an unexpected distribution. One would have expected *diurnu to be a northern innovation, in the Gallo-Romance dialects. Instead, it is found in the southern dialects. c) Some variations (frondea / folium) exist already in Late Latin, and they are not diagnostic for anything.
d) Two villages in Sardinia have conserved Latin album (Romanian alb)
e) Romanian developped alt from altus, high, and alt from alter, other. înalt ”high” got a prefix to fight the homophony.
f) Some words (oleum, acetum, *aciarium, petroselinon, securis) were probably borrowed by Slavic in the VI-VII centuries from the proto-Romance peoples they lived with, maybe the ancestors of Romanians and Arumanians. Some of them (ulei, oil ;oțet, vinegar ; oțel, steel ; pătrunjel, parsley) were borrowed back in Romanian, as French borrowed back from English tennis ( Fr. tenez) and sport (Fr. desport)

Romanian and Gallo-Italic
Introduction
Why compare Romanian with the Italian dialects ?
The Toscan dialect – a beautiful one, with a relatively neutral range of consonants and vowels, easy to pronounce for the Lombards and Sicilians alike, and easy for the foreigners as well has become the official Italian language. But Tuscany covers only 8 % of Italy.
The phonetical, morphological and lexical richness of the other dialects has been neglected in comparisons. From the Latin-sounding Sardinian in the south, to the French-spiced dialects in the north, passing through the menacing dialects of the South and the blasphemous, cursing contests that are the central dialects1, Italy has a rich diversity. However, most handbooks of comparative Romance linguistics focus only on the comparison between literary languages.
The Italian dialects have a rich vocabulary, and share with Romanian archaisms or innovations absent in standard Italian. Comparing the vocabulary of Romanian and Italian dialects is important, as it shows the vocabulary of Latin before the fifth century, when the Barbarian invasions interrupted the continuity of the Romania.
Latin, Sard domus may be opposed to Romanian, Italian, Spanish casa. As Romanian split before the fourth century, we may assume that casa was already general in the II-nd century.
We will compare, in the lines below, the phonetics and the vocabulary of the Romanian language, and the Italian dialects. Some languages – Ladino, Friulano (Furlano) and Sardinian are enumerated, for the sake of brevity, along the dialects. Rumantsch, an independent Romance language, is present as well. There is a dialect continuum between French and Italian. Northern dialects, Genovese, Lombard and Friulan share many phonetic features with French.
There are two isogloss lines, La Spezia – Rimini and Roma – Ancona. North of La SpeziaRimini line are the Gallo-Italian dialects ; in the middle, Toscan and central dialects, “normal Italian language” ; south of the Roma–Ancona line are the southern dialects and Sardinian language.
Difficulties
The present Italian dialects have influenced one another for one thousand years. They have merged and split phonetically many times. The oldest texts – until 1300 – are better sources, but they suffer the other influence – the influence of written Latin.
The first line, the northern line, La Spezia – Rimini, is supposed to separate Gallo-Romance dialects from Toscan, Southern Italian dialects, AND Romanian. In fact, Romanian shares most phonetic shifts with the North. The comparison of Lombard with Romanian may fine-tune the chronology of phonetic shifts in Gallo-Romance.
We have noted the words whose Levenshtein distance is smaller for Romanian vs. dialect than Romanian vs. Italian, or in a different direction.
As all other Romance languages developed in close contact and in permanent contact with Latin, for centuries, Romanian is the only Romance language a) without the influence of written Latin and b)without the influence of another Romance language. It is the only testimony of Latin spoken before the fourth century.

Phonetics.
It will not be always easy to distinguish an old shared phonetical transformation from an independent parallel evolution. (Rom. apă, limbă, patru, iapă / Sard. abba, limba, battor, ebba are independent innovations). The complete palatalization of –LI > -ĭ miel / miei (lamb, lambs) vs. Italian bello/bei or Lombard ul cavèl - i cavèi(hair, hairs) has a common origin.
We have also the plural feminine in –i : cadréga - i cadreghi (chair, chairs) equivalent to the Romanian feminine coardă / corzi (chord, chords) . The many phonetic parallels between Romanian and Lombard may suggest a common origin in Gallo-Italic.
In many cases, the evolution of Romanian and that of Gallo-Italic is independent. But these cases are so many, that we must postulate a common momentum.

The accent in the Gallo-Italic dialects is stronger than in the south.
a) it eliminates the final –o : Rom. mort, Rumantsch, Lombard, Ladin mort, Friulan muart / Toscan morto.

I.3.1 Syncope cum prosthesis
Sometimes, a prosthetic a- is added to repair the syncope of vowels : adman, amsurè, ardusar, armaner, avcjaia, anvoud. The same phenomenon occurs in Abruzzese – arcète (recitare) arcuprì (ricoprire) ardà (ridare) etc. This syncope followed by prosthesis of a- appears in Arumanian alavdu, agrshescu, alumtu, arâboju, aspargu, arcoari, arâi, armân (Rom. român) arâtsirĭ, arvdari, ardzinari, arpâsedzu, aspâreatu. Sometimes the parralel is very good – Romagnolo armanêr ‟to remain”, Arm. armânu ‟to remain”. Of course, the phenomenon – stress, syncope, prosthesis – is an independent development in both languages, but the underlying cause – Gallo-Italic hyperaccentuation – is historically the same.

Phonetics and lexicology
Separing the phonetic observation from the lexical ones is, at times, impossible.
Romanian aripă “wing” is secondary. It does not come from Lat. ala, who eroded in protoRomanian to aa. We know this, thanks to Lat. ala > Ligurian aa. Romanian albină “bee” does not come from Latin apis, who gave proto-Romanian aa. We know this thanks to Lombard aa “bee” and Ladin é. Romanian has inimă “heart” < Lat. anima “soul” because Lat. cor “heart” gave Romanian *cur, who entered in an embarassing homonymy with cur “asshole”. We know this thanks to Ladin cûr “heart”. picior “foot” has replaced a short pe (present in Gallo-Italic dialects) which entered in homonymy with pe “on”. Ladin oio “oil” explains why Romanian has borrowed ulei from Slavic. *oi was too short and entered in homonymy with oi “sheep”. Ladin moee “to move” explains why Romanian has replaced it with a mișca. Romanian has only păr “hair” not capelli/cheveux ; the frail Bergamasco caèi “hair” explains this replacement.
Why did iecur ”liver” disappear from Latin ? It is not an unimportant word; it is usually kept in most Indo-European languages ; it is a vital organ and a central term. Its disappearance deserves an explanation. Appendix Probi, the III-rd century text, has iecur non iocur. *iocu- ”liver” entered in homonymy with *iocu- ”game, play” and was, therefore, replaced.
The Italian dialects are helpful not only in comparing words that exist in Romanian, but they explain as well why certain words disappeared and were replaced.
Alter and altus may evolve to enter in homonymy, as in Piemontese. The situation appeared very early and was solved very early – we have Romanian înalt, Occitan naut (and Occ. mai naut, Rom. mai înalt ) There is no homonymy in the Italian dialects.
plecare ”to come or go” exists in Romanian and the Peregrinatio Aetheriae :
”et sic plecaremus nos ad montem”.
Extinct Dalmatian shares with Romanian maura/mare “big” and tuota/tata “father”. However, Dalmatian has stayed in contact with Italian until it disappeared, and has many common features with Italian dialects, either genetic or borrowed.
Latin mus has hidden itself, no pun intended. It still exists in Veneto (Trevisano Onigo morìcia “mouse” but was replaced elsewhere by sorex, itself replaced by topo in Toscan).
Romanian and the Alpine Lombard dialect
Romanian shares many phonetic characteristics with the Alpine Lombard from the Canton Ticino. Alpine Lombard did not undergo the Standard Italian (Toscan) influence present in Western Lombard. Of course, many of these phonetic features appear in one or another Italian dialects as well, but they form a cluster in Alpine Lombard.

Vocabulary
Italian has kept a permanent relation with Latin, either through Church or the University. Latin is an adstrate of Italian. Some inherited words are kept in dialects, while Italian has replaced them with some Latinate loanword. Thus, in many cases, the testimony of dialects is more important than Italian itself.
a) some names ( a piece, pezzo ; boy, ragazzo; un poco, a little ) and verbs (to take, prendere; to work, lavorare, to find, trovare) are present in our list not for the sake of comparison, but to show the diversity of the synonyms used. These words are frequent in the language, but their synchronic frequence is not proportional with their diachronic durability. Language has decided to use for some concepts (name, foot, moon, water, star, night) everlasting names, and to use disposable words for other concepts (child, to take). Spanish has llevar, coger, tomar, Italian prendere, čapar, tor, pigliar, Rom. a lua, all replacing Lat. capio. The meaning [to take] is represented by unstable words. [to work] has different names in the Italian dialects. Romanian has added a munci “to work” from Slavic not because it needed a new word, but because the meaning is intrinsically unstable. It also has its own intensity treadmill : travailler comes from tripalium, torture, as a munci does ; fatecà means “to get tired” before “to work” : the language replaces the word for working with another denoting exhausting exertion, which in turn loses intensity and is replaced.
b) Week-day names (lunedi, martedi, mercoledi) may have been influenced by Germanic (Montag, Diens-tag, Wednes-tag) or not ; Romanian, Spanish, Sard and many Italian dialects keep the simplified form – luni, marti etc.
dies and *diurnu (northern di, Sard. die) have an unexpected distribution. One would have expected *diurnu to be a northern innovation, in the Gallo-Romance dialects, because it exists in French. Instead, it is found in the southern dialects.
c) Some pairs (frondea/folium ; *viridia/caulis ; faber/*ferrarius ; caseum/*formaticum ; dextra/*directa ) existed already in Late Latin, and none has eliminated the other. Although we mentioned them in our table, they have no diagnostic value.
d) Rumantsch and three villages in Sardinia have conserved Latin album (Romanian alb)
e) Romanian developped alt from altus, high, and alt from alter, other. înalt “high” got a prefix to fight the homophony.
f) Some words (oleum, acetum, *aciarium, petroselinon, securis, cerasus) were probably borrowed by Slavic in the VI-VII centuries from the proto-Romance peoples they were mixed with or neighbor to, most probably the ancestors of Romanians and Arumanians.



Some of them (ulei, oil ; oțet, vinegar ; oțel, steel ; pătrunjel, parsley) were borrowed back in Romanian, as French borrowed back from English tennis ( Fr. tenez) and sport (Fr. desport).
g) Some words – cu toate că “despite”, totdeauna “always” belong to a trash category. The category they belong to (indefinite pronouns ; conjunctions) are unstable, different in all Romance languages, independent creations in each language. However, the first has equivalents in Puglia, and the second, in North Lombard. This suggest that the classic Latin system – quamquam, quamvis / semper etc. collapsed very soon, maybe in the second century AD, and these conjunctions and indefinitie pronouns were replaced by paraphrases, either local, or a magma of numerous synonyms, one of which prevailed finally in one area.
h) Romanian shares words with French that are not attested in Italy. *impromutuare “to borrow” has Fr. emprunter, Rom. a împrumuta. In Italian it has a different meaning – ”to promise”. Geoffrey Stephen Hull, (The Linguistic Unity of Northern Italy and Rhaetia, Ph. D., p. 13) observes that Gallo-Italic separates words with the prefix dis- from prefix ex-, while Toscan prefers the second ones. Romanian belongs typologically to Gallo-Italic here. Fr. décharger, découdre, découvrir and Rom. a descărca, a descoase, a descoperi vs. Toscan scaricare, scuciare, scoprire. However, the Gallo-Italic lexic identified by Hull does not exist in Romanian. Another Gallo-Italic feature, the sonorization of intervocalic voiceless consonants (scutum > scudo, vitellus > vedel ) does not exist in Romanian. The typical vocabulary and the sonorization did not appear before the fourth century, when Romanian was already separated.
i) Romanian does not have the indefinite pronouns It. ne, ci ̧Fr. en, y, and the partitive article del pane / du pain. They are late. Also, it does not form adverbs in –mente, as South Italic. The only adverb so formed, altminteri <*altera-menter points to a suffix –menter, a hybrid between mente and Class. Lat. –enter – libenter, volenter. It existed and was productive in Old Spanish as mientre. A possible, yet teleologic, explanation, of this avoidance, was that it entered in a confusing homonymy with –mentum îmbrăcăminte, acoperămînt, învățămînt, jurămînt, legămînt, used to create names.

It was just an accident that these marginal Toscan forms who entered the literary language. All dialects have vacca, porco, only Toscan has replaced them by mucca and maiale (maiale is early, mucca very late). All dialects have sorex, Toscan only has topo.
