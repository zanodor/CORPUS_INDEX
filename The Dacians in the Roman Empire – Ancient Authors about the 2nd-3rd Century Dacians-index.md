Argument
Some general works that deal with Antiquity-related topics are naturally accompanied by corpora of ancient texts that aim at supporting the analysis with direct references. For the history of Dacia, the first collection of literary sources is Fontes ad historiam Dacoromaniae pertinentes,2 consisting of four volumes published by a team of researchers from the Romanian Academy. The first volume comprises written sources related to the ancient history of Romania, from Hesiod until the end of the 3rd century and the second volume collects texts dated between 300 and 1000 AD.3 Some texts from the second interval make reference to Dacia and its population during the 2nd and 3rd centuries. In the third volume one finds 11th–14th-century Byzantine authors who were also inspired in their historical works by the texts of historians from Classical or Late Antiquity. D. Ruscu’s Provincia Dacia în istoriografia antică4 [The province of Dacia in ancient historiography] analyzes the political and military history of Dacia between the Roman conquest and the abandonment of the province from the perspective of the historical sources. Brief incursion in the universe of the ancient texts and their authors
I have placed Statius and Martial, contemporary poets, among the first in the chronological list of authors that mention the Dacians in passing. Publius Papinius Statius, born in Neapolis, where his father ran a school as grammaticus, inherited his father’s talent for the letters. In Silvae (“Pieces of raw material”), a collection of poems, and in the epic poem Thebaid, he included mentions of the Dacians. Thus, in a few verses he describes the victory over the Dacians that Domitian celebrated with a triumph (Silvae 3.3.117–118), still proving his clemency by letting the Dacians have their mountain – quaeque suum Dacis donat clementia montem (Silvae 3.3.169).16
Martial, a poet from Hispania (born in Bilbilis, Hispania Tarraconensis), lived between ca. 38/41 and 104. Though his parents were not wealthy, he received a good education and moved to Rome. At his death Pliny the Younger, touched by the poet’s passing, wrote the following lines: “But one might say his writings will not last. Perhaps they will not, but he wrote as if they would”.17 He became established by writing epigrams, a type of literary production in verses that expresses in a concentrated manner an idea, an aspect of a person, an event, or that makes reference to a special object. Everyday scenes spice up Martial’s epigrams. He is extremely talented and critical, a good observer of everyday life, though his indecency and adulation of Domitian take away a bit of his glory. Both these negative traits can be noted in the restricted selection that I have made here. In epigram no. 22 from book I Martial provides an allegorical evocation of the Dacians18 symbolically represented by a hare placed in front of a lion during an arena show. The scene is described through a successful combination of figures of speech – allegory, synecdoche, and antithesis – in order to suggest the greatness of the strong as opposed to the weak. The show was organized by Diocletian19 who invited various guests and as host provided them with an event with a historical lesson by allegorically symbolizing emperor Domitian through a lion and the Dacian child through a hare. Martian evokes the emperor’s forgiving attitude, his generosity, but also a certain disregard for the weak who is not worthy of consideration as he means nothing when compared to the power of the strong. The emperor’s power is amplified by the hare’s fear, as it flees despite the fact that the lion does not attack – placidi ora leonis. Sources about the Dacian peoples
Based on the writings of ancient authors regarding the Dacian peoples of the 2nd–3rd centuries, one can identify several groups of topics. 1. The distinction between Dacians and Getae
The older sources (e.g.: Str. 7.3.13; Plin. HN. 4.80) reflect this lexicographic distinction60 determined foremost by their being part of either the Latin or the Greek tradition, but also by the geographic location of the two peoples, without indications of ethnic differences between them. One notes that the distinction persists in the writings of authors active starting with the 2nd century, but we should keep in mind the fact that they sometimes adopt pieces of information from older sources. Thus in Dionys. Per. 304–307 one encounters the enumeration of the tribes inhabiting the northern parts, including both the Dacians and the Getae: (...) Γερμανοὶ Σαρμάται τε Γέται ʼθ ἅμα Βαστάρναι τε, Δακῶν τʼ ἄσπετος αἶα(...). In his work entitled Catholic Prosody Herodian, a 2nd-century grammarian, quotes Strabo in saying that the inhabitants on the side of the Pontus are called Getae and those on the opposite side are called “dai”: «Γέτας γὰρ τοὺς πρὸς τὸν Πόντον κεκλιμένους καὶ τὴν ἕω, Δάους δὲ τοὺς πρὸς τἀναντία πρὸς Γερμανίαν καὶ τὰς τοῦ Ἴστρου πηγάς ». 2. Dacian society
During the reign of Decebalus, Dacian society appears as a community of free people consisting of at least two categories: a wealthier group, “the nobility” that according to Jordanes, who was quoting Dio Chrysostom, was initially formed of tarabostesei, and then of pilleati (Jord. Get. V, 40), among which the leaders and the priests were elected; and a second group, the common people, those with long hair, whom Jordanes says Dicineus decided to be called capillati – reliquam vero gentem capillatos dicere iussit (Jord. Get. XI, 72). The pileus, an element of Dacian dress that distinguished them from other peoples, is the word that generated the name of the noble group. During Trajan’s First Dacian War, the emissaries Decebalus sent to the emperor were pileati, not comati, as they were in the first delegation, according to Cass. Dio 68. 9. 1: οὐκέτι τῶν κομητῶν ὥσπερ πρότερον, ἀλλὰ τῶν πιλοφόρων τοὺς ἀρίστους. In a speech apparently held in Rome after his return from exile, Dio Chrysostom noted the presence of various ethnic groups in the city, identifying them based on peculiarities of their dress (Dio Chrys. Or. 72.3.4). The Getae he saw πίλους ἐπὶ ταῖς κεφαλαῖς ἔχοντας, wearing pilei on their heads.

Conclusions
Ancient literature describes the Dacians/Getae as a people inhabiting a far away, northern, and cold land, where even the stars froze under the harshness of the Hyperborean constellation.66 The Dacian typical home, located on a peak, domus ardua Daci (Stat. Silv. 1.1.7) suggests the perfect image of the lands they inhabited. In his turn, Florus tells that: Daci montibus inhaerent (Flor. 2.28.2). Domitian himself, in his clemency, left the Dacians their mountain – suum Dacis donat clementia montem (Stat. Silv. 3.3.169). The Romans were also chilled by the sight of the frozen Danube, as the northern barbarians crossed it during their plunder and pillage incursions into Roman lands (Plin. Pan. 12.3; Paus. 8.28; Cass. Dio 68.13.6; Flor. 2.28.2–4). The climate aided them in their fight more than their weapons – cum ferae gentes non telis magis quam suo caelo, suo sidere armantur (Plin. Pan. 12.3).
I have discussed a rather large number of sources, some of which mention Domitian’s conflicts with the Dacians or Trajan’s wars67 and others deal with the abandonment of Dacia. In general, these texts that narrate political and military events are propagandistic in nature, exaggerating the descriptions precisely in order to stress Roman greatness. The braver and wilder the vanquished barbarians and the more unfavorable the battleground, the more precious the victory of the Romans and the more praiseworthy the emperor. Thus, many of these paragraphs are in fact clichés.

Not many sources on the Dacians remain if one excludes the texts that mention the wars and focuses strictly on the narrations dealing with the period of the Dacian province. For the 2nd–3rd centuries one can turn to a few paragraphs in the works of geographers and cartographers such as Balbus and Dionysius Periegetes, to Lucius Ampelius’ encyclopedia, and to Herodian’s linguistic considerations that include references to the Dacians. The statements of the grammarians must be approached cautiously as they can adopt data from other authors or make general references, without any chronological mention. Such general evaluations of the Dacians – of their warrior character, their religiosity, but also their wisdom – have been preserved from Lucian, Clement, Philostratus, and Origen. One piece of evidence dealing with the incursion of the Free Dacians into the territory of the province of Dacia during the reign of emperor Macrinus (218) has been preserved in the writings of Cassius Dio (79.27). Late antique authors mainly deal with Dacia as a province, focusing on its abandonment. A paragraph from Festus (8) mentions that the Romans were moved south of the Danube. SHA 39.7 says that both the army and the provincials were left – exercitu et provincialibus reliquit.
In conclusion one might say that chroniclers, historians, and men of letters were interested by the Dacians especially as a barbarian population who posed a potential threat at the empire’s border. Their interest seems to have faded once the Dacians were conquered and joined, as provincials, the great mass of the empire’s population. More precise pieces of information must be searched among the epigraphic sources and in the results of archaeological excavations. One must also note that the vision of the historians is sometimes influenced by political trends and personal beliefs. In other cases they have adopted pieces of information from previous sources, without critically evaluating them. The picture of the cosmopolitan world of the great city that Dio Crysostom describes (Dio Chrys. Or. 72.3.4), a city that brings together the peoples of the world with their diverse costumes and habits and where the Getae are only individualized through the characteristic cap they wear (pileus), may be the one that foreshadows a society in which the provincials are integrated in the standards of the Empire.

Hence Virgil says: “and Father Gradivus who rules the Getic fields”.139 Now Mars has always been worshipped by the Goths with cruel rites, and captives were slain as his victims. They thought that he who is the lord of war needed to be appeased by the shedding of human blood. To him they devoted the first share of the spoil, and in his honor arms stripped from the foe were suspended from trees. And they had more than all other races a deep spirit of religion, since the worship of this god seemed to be really bestowed upon their ancestor.

Thus he started a war against the Dacians, whom the Ionians call Dakoi, as Apian says in the 23rd book of the Roman History, because he did not want to give them the money they took annually and he wished to punish them for their deeds. Therefore Decebalus, who was reigning over the Dacians, finding out about the expedition initiated by Trajan against his people, was afraid, because he knew that the man was very good at military affairs. As the battle raged between them, the Romans killed many enemies, and no fewer of theirs were wounded. There were so many wounded that missing the bandages, Trajan did not spare his own coat. Trajan, braking up the camp, took also the delegates of Decebalus to Italy. They were introduced into the senate, and, laying down their arms and clasping their hands as prisoners do, they said many things and they begged. After receiving treaties, they took up arms again. Trajan got the honors of triumph and was named Dacicus. And he did not neglect other matters due to the war and did no less justice, but he did it in many places and often judged from the rostrum. When he was told that Decebalus was rebelling again and neglecting the treaties, he went to war with him once more. Decebalus, as he was inferior in strength, carefully arranged to defeat him by deception. And knowing that Trajan is a kind man, and that he receives the one who wants to talk to him, he sent some supposed deserters who would kill him, if possible. But one of them was suspected and caught and, under investigation, confessed the secret. After building a bridge over the Ister, passing across a work above all admiration and unequalled, the emperor crossed the river on this bridge and defeated the Dacians, hardly and facing dangers, but he defeated them. Decebalus, losing his mind, committed suicide. Since then the Dacian people and their country have become subject to the Romans. And he also found the treasure of Decebalus, although this discovery was difficult to make. For the barbarous, having diverted from his bed a river flowing near his capital, had dug very deep into the earth over which the water passed, and had placed in the pit much gold and still more silver and other precious things which are not destroyed by moisture and covered the pit with slabs and put earth and gravel on top and thus again brought the water back to the previous bed.
